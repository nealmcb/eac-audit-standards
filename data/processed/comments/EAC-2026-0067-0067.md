Comments on Election Audit Standards, Docket EAC-2026-0067 April 27, 2026 Introduction I have experience in conducting many types of audits, including ISO-9001, ISO 10000, ISO 27001, CMMI-DEV, CMMI-SVC, and Federal government audits of processes, police departments, and research facilities. I have managed Federal ISSO cybersecurity assessments of major IT systems using NIST SP800-53a as a control reference. I have conducted extensive research on US Election Systems, including developing risk management frameworks, work breakdown structures, and security controls to mitigate threats. Background The US Election System is comprised of 646 distinct processes covering 57 major areas under the pre-election, election, and post-election major domains. Written procedures for these processes vary from state to state. In order to assess the accuracy of an election, states should use auditing of the election processes, equipment (tabulators and databases), data (ballots and records) and compliance (were the tasks performed correctly). The EAC has developed a draft auditing standard to accommodate the states in federal elections. Analysis In order to reduce the risk of errors in federal elections, audits can be prescribed. Different types of audits are required for each major election domain. The domains include: 1. process audit, 2. equipment audit, 3. data audit, 4. compliance audit. Standards are in place that define the best practices for auditing, including confidentiality, accountability, transparency, timeliness, independence, controls, risk assessment, and remediation. These best practices should be incorporated into the EAC final audit standards. Recommendations 1. Audit standards should be listed and sortable not only by topic, but by type of audit and by election process sequence (before, during, after election). The standards should be traceable to established auditing standards for the appropriate domain. 2. The auditing process should not be owned by the state CIO, Secretary of State, or Elections Office. It should be a separate entity under the Governor’s office, normally residing in an Inspector General’s office. The authority should be shared by representatives from political parties participating in the election. All auditing processes should be visible to observers from the respective political parties. 3. Auditors should be credentialed in the type of audit being conducted, and experienced in auditing procedures related to elections. 4. Defects discovered in the auditing process should be listed as incidents, and classified as major (affecting the count) or minor (affecting the process). They should be investigated to determine the root cause. The defect should be corrected, and associated processes improved in order to preclude the recurrence of the defect. 5. In order to maintain consistency and identification of issues, auditing of elections should refer to the specific processes involved in accordance with the US election work breakdown structure (WBS) of 646 processes (NIST Election Model of 2019). I can provide a copy of this WBS. Conclusion The EAC Election Auditing Guide is a great start to defining a standard set of processes for US federal election audits. The recommendations above can strengthen the guide and ensure a high level of maturity in the auditing processes.
