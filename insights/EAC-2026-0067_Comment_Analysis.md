## How to read this document

This report has four parts:

1. **Summary table** of all 71 submissions — submitter, affiliation, contact information where provided, and a one-line summary.
2. **Detailed entries** expanding each submission into a short readable paragraph.
3. **Topical synthesis** — where commenters broadly agree, and where they are in tension.
4. **Conclusion.**

A note on data: the docket numbers its comments roughly EAC-2026-0067-0002 through -0072 (there is no -0001), which is exactly 71 records. The CSV/JSON metadata for this docket contains only the submitter's name and organization; it does **not** carry a contact-info field, so a commenter's email, phone, or mailing address appears only when the person typed it into the body of their own submission. The table therefore shows contact details for the handful of (mostly organizational) commenters who included them, and "Not provided" otherwise. Four brief submissions — IDs **-0007, -0009, -0017, and -0024** — could not be individually recovered from the project's text index; they are listed in their numeric place and flagged. One of these four is a near-empty submission whose entire text is "`?????`".

## 2. Detailed entries

**0002 — Ronald Rivest.** Calls the draft "an excellent draft report" but identifies four gaps: it lacks any discussion of statistical audits such as risk-limiting audits (RLAs) and how to explain their results to a non-technical audience; it does not note that a statistical audit's workload varies with chance and with the closeness of the contest; it gives no advice on choosing which contests to audit when resources are limited; and it offers no guidance on selecting ballots at random.

**0003 — Stephanie Singer.** Proposes one concrete addition to the table "Example: Auditing Activities Before, During, and After an Election" — an "Anomaly Audit," defined as an analysis of election data to identify statistical anomalies, followed by investigation of any significant ones.

**0004 — Michelle Burchill.** Writes as someone who has worked in elections for several years; says the work has increased her trust in the system but that there should still be more auditing, since many jurisdictions audit comprehensively only when something has gone wrong. She values outside expertise and especially likes the option of a separate tabulation audit that checks all ballots rather than a small sample.

**0005 — Civera.** A company that markets "ElectionShield," a tool that uses statistics to detect anomalies or irregularities in results. It urges that statistical/forensic analysis of results become a standard security practice nationwide so that detected anomalies can be investigated for root cause, citing the ballot-stuffing conviction of a former U.S. congressman as an example of what such analysis can surface.

**0006 — Gregory Lawson.** Praises the committee's work but finds the document too long and too "in the weeds." Proposes that each state and county create an Audit Committee (current and former election officials plus members-at-large) on a rotating two-year basis that would hire an out-of-state independent auditor, rotating the auditor each cycle. Suggests the audit cover the election system, machine/signature accuracy, registration, and post-election tabulation, and floats the idea of a training program for a new profession of independent election auditors.

**0007 — *(not retrievable).*** This submission exists in the docket but could not be isolated from the project's search index; its submitter and text are unavailable here.

**0008 — Julie Hall.** Says the audit should be done by an independent third party, or by the State Auditor, who has the staff and mission to audit — letting the Secretary of State perform it is "putting the hen in charge of the hen house."

**0009 — *(not retrievable).*** Exists in the docket but could not be isolated from the project index.

**0010 — dave cantera.** A one-line submission: auditing in any industry is based on independent observation by a disinterested party or organization.

**0011 — Denise Flohr.** Audits must be done independently and forensically; auditing oneself is, in her view, beyond the current "state of morals." She frames independent auditing as the minimum needed to assure voters their votes count.

**0012 — Ohio Election Integrity Network.** Strongly urges that states perform independent audits, calling secure, accurate, and honest audits a must for voter confidence.

**0013 — Stephen Aleshire.** Wants a disinterested third party at arm's length from poll workers, data aggregators, secretaries of state, and candidates. Distinctively, he criticizes risk-limiting audits as "statistically irrelevant and in fact misleading," and argues that counting paper ballots using the same poll workers or candidates is invalid.

**0014 — jayne hall.** Audits should be done by a third party with no affiliation to the county election board, and the Secretary of State should not be involved either.

**0015 — Marilyn Roggenkamp.** Argues for independent auditors rather than someone from the Secretary of State's office — not as a judgment on the SoS, but for the sake of transparency.

**0016 — Anonymous.** Politely requests independent, objective third-party audits — auditors with no special interest in the subject, as far removed as possible from the work product and free of undue bias.

**0017 — *(not retrievable).*** Exists in the docket but could not be isolated from the project index. (One of the four un-retrieved submissions is a near-empty entry whose text is simply "?????"; it is likely one of these.)

**0018 — Christine Welborn.** It is critical that an independent auditor, not the Secretary of State's office, audit elections; the auditor needs enough knowledge of election systems but must be an outsider to ensure accuracy and transparency.

**0019 — Diane Vernesoni.** Ballot auditing after an election should be independent, and results must reconcile votes cast against ballots distributed. Any result that does not reconcile should not be accepted as a true result; correct precinct results are paramount.

**0020 — Gregory Lawson.** (Lawson's second submission.) Audits should be performed by a party independent of the County Auditor's office — at minimum the State Auditor, and ideally two separate out-of-state parties.

**0021 — Lori Wieber.** A short statement that audits must be done independently and not by those who run the elections, framed as key to the integrity of results and to trust.

**0022 — Julie Adams.** Audits must be done INDEPENDENTLY; audits done by election employees, officials, vendors, or state election officials are "not real audits." She would have them performed by a credible audit firm or state auditors.

**0023 — Anonymous.** Independent agency or third party, separate from the administrator. The vendor paid to report totals should not verify its own reporting, and the Secretary of State who runs elections should not also audit them or adjudicate complaints — illustrated with an extended "henhouse fox" metaphor.

**0024 — *(not retrievable).*** Exists in the docket but could not be isolated from the project index.

**0025 — Jody Hall.** Strongly urges that all audits be conducted by a certified third party.

**0026 — Sharla Blakely.** A one-line statement that any and all audits must be conducted by a third party.

**0027 — Cheryl Warren.** Supports third-party audits and recounts a personal experience filing seven FOIA requests with a local Texas election administrator, who reportedly said she couldn't access the information because the Secretary of State's numbers didn't match hers — leaving the commenter questioning what is happening in Texas.

**0028 — Marie Wood.** All audits should be conducted by a third party.

**0029 — Kevin McCall.** All audits should be conducted by a third-party entity.

**0030 — Robin Hayes.** Emphatically: all audits should be done by a third party.

**0031 — Rosario Zarb-Cousin.** All election audits should be done by a third party to ensure they are non-partisan and transparent, with a report that states scope, testing criteria, and results and is made public.

**0032 — Rosario Zarb-Cousin.** (Second submission.) Adds that an independent third-party audit should occur at least annually and should include review or testing of general and security controls of election-system vendors, with scope, criteria, testing results, recommendations, and a summary of actions taken.

**0033 — Melinda Roberts.** Third party only, with no connection to any election vendor; objects that current audits are "run with ES&S in the room" helping to set them up and create the test — "the fox guarding the hen house."

**0034 — Monica Childers.** The entire submission is the word "Test," indicating a test post rather than a substantive comment.

**0035 — bill Mahavier.** Audits are not fair and accurate unless done by a third party.

**0036 — Vanessa Alva.** Audits should be conducted by a third party and allow public observers to ensure proper handling of ballots.

**0037 — Gary Chick.** Reads the draft as letting election departments audit themselves; argues audits should be required by law and performed by third parties, as in regulated industries such as publicly traded companies and government-grant recipients. Notes that auditors elsewhere already handle sensitive data, and asks the EAC to stress the confidence third-party audits provide.

**0038 — Richard Koury.** A seven-point program: an independent audit committee (separate from those running elections or maintaining voter rolls); auditor access to all records from original paper ballots through cast vote records with chain-of-custody data; ballot-accounting tests matching voters to votes against the official record; documented and secured findings for future reference; valid statistical methods set by the committee; trained auditors; and a committee report to each county.

**0039 — Chris Bystroff.** Argues the draft's most consequential gap is that it never specifies what must happen when an audit finds a discrepancy. He proposes three principles: discrepancies are resolved only by documentary evidence (not mere explanation); the burden of demonstrating resolution rests on the administering authority, not candidates or the public; and the determination should not rest solely with the authority whose systems produced the discrepancy — favoring a bipartisan/nonpartisan body or judicial review. He ties this to the draft's own Impartiality, Independence, and Efficacy standards and to RLA methodology.

**0040 — Anonymous.** Supports national audit standards as a way to give the public evidence that outcomes match the ballots cast without making voting harder. Urges a bigger emphasis on plain public language and short public summaries of method and conclusions, and warns that procedures must not become so complex that small jurisdictions can't carry them out.

**0041 — Robert Kufert.** Calls it an excellent general document but deficient because it doesn't target the areas most prone to fraud — vote-by-mail, UOCAVA (overseas) ballots, catching non-citizens at registration, and removing already-registered non-citizens. Recommends enhanced controls on envelope/ballot production, intake security, storage, reconciliation, voter ID, UOCAVA address verification, and voter-roll cleanup.

**0042 — Anonymous.** Offers standard-by-standard suggestions on the Objective topic: questions whether the chief election official should direct an audit when that official is on the ballot; suggests allowing private funding as a safeguarded fallback when public funding falls short; and recommends rewording the Impartiality standard to acknowledge that everyone carries some bias, focusing instead on requiring factual evidence over assumption or hearsay.

**0043 — Doug Befort.** Argues that election officials have wide, untransparent discretion and that he knows of no election office subject to periodic independent audits — so the public cannot know whether offices do a good job. Says the EAC's voluntary standards do not actually provide for independence, because an audit officials plan, execute, and report is not independent. Lists many processes (internal controls, registration processing, list maintenance, cybersecurity, vote-by-mail handling, results aggregation and reporting, records requests, ballot storage) that should be independently audited.

**0044 — Michigan Fair Elections Institute (Patrice Johnson, Chair & Founder).** Submits substantive text plus an attachment endorsing four key reforms — an independent audit committee, enhanced county-level reporting, full auditor access to artifacts, and substantive financial/compliance audits of HAVA grant recipients — and asks the EAC to provide model language that state legislatures can adopt. Contact: P.O. Box 41, Stockbridge, MI 49285; 517-420-7978; patrice@mifairelections.org.

**0045 — Richard Vermeers.** Fully supports Michael Raisch's (0056) comments and argues that, given rising reports of state-level fraud and weak state-auditor responses, audits should obviously be independent rather than internal.

**0046 — John Cardarelli.** For accuracy, a hand count must be compared against the machine count; the hand count could be performed by bipartisan, reputable local people chosen by a random procedure such as a lottery.

**0047 — Mark Vaeth.** Writes as a CPA with an MBA and 25+ years as a Department of Defense supervisory auditor. Endorses the MFEI reforms and Raisch's edits, stressing that independence is foundational under GAGAS (the "Yellow Book") and that self-auditing fails the basic test of objectivity. Adds a call for required statistical sampling of precincts, ballots, voter records, and poll books, and for real financial accountability for HAVA funds (suspension, claw-back) when states fall short.

**0048 — Steven Work.** Off-topic relative to audit standards; argues against universal suffrage and for a "Civic Responsibility and Community Engagement Act" that would make voting and office-holding contingent on four years of community or military service, with numerous external links. Not a comment on the audit standards themselves.

**0049 — Victoria Cruz.** Praises the principle-based framework and especially the Independence standard, but notes most local offices lack full-time audit experts. Recommends that the standards point jurisdictions to existing resources — credentialed independent election auditors (e.g., the Institute of Election Auditors' CEAs), Verified Voting's audit team, and state-level independent auditors — and suggests adding sample RFP/contract language and a "Resources for Implementation" section.

**0050 — Jeff Schaeper.** Writes as a retired finance manager and current Oakland County, Michigan canvasser. Says the latest draft is much improved but needs clarity: a different group (e.g., an Inspector General or Auditor General) should audit to satisfy segregation of duties; all election artifacts must be available; some audits must occur during certification (timeliness); and states without a robust audit process should lose HAVA eligibility until compliant. Frames it in accounting terms (materiality, timeliness, GAGAS/GAAP) and contrasts meaningful audits with "check the box" ones.

**0051 — Gregory Buck.** Three specific edits: revise the "Compliance or Procedural Audit" description so monitoring covers registration, list maintenance, and related activities "throughout the year," not just the election; place audits under an independent audit committee reporting to the legislature rather than under the chief election official; and correct the "Differences in Audit Staffing" table, where the "Non-Partisan" description wrongly equates non-disclosure of bias with neutrality.

**0052 — Marleen Laska.** A one-line request that the EAC incorporate the changes proposed by Michael Raisch, CPA.

**0053 — America First Legal Foundation (Alice Kass, Attorney).** Supports the standards as a step toward election integrity but argues they contain significant omissions and must add audit guidance in three areas: voter-citizenship verification (with a long catalog of state examples and a HAVA-based authority argument), ballot drop-box security (surveillance, tamper-evident seals, patrols, bipartisan chain-of-custody logs), and signature verification (separately reporting missing vs. non-matching signatures, cure procedures, and automated-system accuracy review). Contact: 611 Pennsylvania Ave SE #231, Washington, DC 20003; (202) 964-3721; alice.kass@aflegal.org.

**0054 — Eileen Watts.** Three suggestions: shift audit administration from entities within the election system to an independent audit committee; add reporting on county performance during the audit beyond mere verification of results; and ensure auditors have access to all related artifacts with voter-confidentiality protections.

**0055 — Honest Elections Project.** A nonpartisan nonprofit that supports the standards, citing its polling that 95% of registered voters want transparent, accountable elections and 86% support routine review and audits. Acknowledges the decentralization of U.S. elections and the many audit types, and frames the central challenge as striking the right balance between flexibility and consistency in core audit principles. (Submission includes two specific suggestions in its attachment.)

**0056 — Michael Raisch.** A semi-retired Colorado CPA whose overall impression is very positive but whose central concern is independence — which he calls the most important standard, because without it trust collapses and the audit becomes "a meaningless ceremonial exercise." His key point: the draft addresses independence of the people doing the audit but not the bigger conflict that the Chief Election Official controls the rulemaking over the audit. He recommends each state stand up an independent election commission (state auditor, SoS, clerks, CPAs, lawyers, statisticians, etc.) and produce a management report, and attaches a redlined draft plus a parallel policy memo to Colorado's Secretary of State.

**0057 — MTX Group.** Applauds the publication and offers a single edit: on the Public Observation discussion (page 31), delete the clause "attendance is often low even when observation is offered," arguing it doesn't convey the positives of observation or match the document's overall tenor.

**0058 — Verified Voting (Mark Lindeman, Policy & Strategy Director).** Supports the voluntary standards as guideposts rather than hurdles. Refines the independence concept — the point is to prevent situations where perceived bias could invalidate results, which matters less if auditors clearly cannot dictate outcomes. Urges stronger language on random sampling ("protocols should be carefully designed to ensure fair and representative samples") and flags minor editorial issues (a likely editing error in the impartiality discussion, a cut-off text box, and a "Complie Results" typo in a flow chart).

**0059 — The Elections Group.** A team of election experts that offers strong, standard-by-standard support across authorization, ethics, funding, impartiality, independence, competence, standardization, security, public communication, and transparency. Draws on its 2024 study (with academic researchers) finding that audit communications focused on the *process* most increased voter confidence, and supports credentialing/training, standardized checklists, and plain-language public reporting. (Two near-identical attachment versions.)

**0060 — Anonymous.** A single technical assertion: no election equipment with a modem or internet connection can be certified if real certification is intended.

**0061 — Kurt Hyde.** Proposes a candidate-driven audit-selection model: any candidate receiving at least 10% of the vote in a district of 75,000+ registered voters could select one precinct to audit, with a paper-ballot recount for that contest and an optional check that the recorded voters exist and voted. Such audits should be open to the public without training or appointments, with video and audio allowed.

**0062 — Citizens Oversight, Inc. (Raymond Lutz, Executive Director).** A critical-but-constructive submission that treats the draft as a principles document needing operational teeth. Its central proposal is the "Public Transparency Protocol" (PTP) — scripted, in-view, real-time procedures (for L&A testing, batch-comparison audits, RLAs, ballot collection/chain-of-custody, hash verification, and backups) that observers can verify and reproduce. It also proposes defining "Ballot Image Audits," unifying chain-of-custody with reconciliation into a "Ballot Accounting Audit," requiring structured human- and machine-readable records, and asking NIST to develop cryptographic protection for ballot images. (Two attachments, including the PTP procedures.)

**0063 — John Droz.** A detailed, numbered technical critique with many recommendations: add standards for Timeliness, Accuracy, and Statistical Soundness; have the EAC acknowledge the fallibility of the machines it certifies and add a "Software Independent Verification" standard requiring results to be confirmable by a manual hand count; sharply distinguish "procedural audits" from "verification audits"; rename "Flexibility" to avoid lowering standards; define "Meaningful Observation"; add an "Audit Transparency Profile," "Negative Ballot Transparency," and "Discrepancy Reporting." (Submitted as an attachment.)

**0064 — Enhanced Voting.** Argues that public-sector auditing standards already exist and are well-tested (INTOSAI internationally; the GAO domestically), so the EAC defining its own standards is both duplicative and, given the trust deficit, risky. Recommends the EAC instead define the specific audit *needs* of U.S. elections and anchor to existing, trusted standards rather than creating broad new ones.

**0065 — William Kresse.** A law/accounting professor and former Chicago Board of Elections commissioner who applauds the draft and its Objective/Professional/Effective/Secure/Accountable structure. His central proposal: credential those who conduct or oversee election audits, so the public is assured (through education, training, background, and testing) that audits are in competent hands — which also creates a mechanism to discipline auditors who violate professional standards.

**0066 — Paul Burke.** Suggests adding a definition of "Ballot Image Audit" (used statewide in GA, MD, SC), distinct from the existing "Automated independent ballot audit"; allowing both internal and external audits (as in financial auditing) with reports that disclose who performed them; and creating standardized audit reports (dates, topics, discrepancies, extent, limitations, methods, who appointed auditors, team composition, links) to enable future EAVS/policy-survey data collection. Contact: admin@VoteWell.net.

Says auditing should not be owned by the CIO, SoS, or elections office but by a separate entity (e.g., an Inspector General) with shared party oversight; auditors should be credentialed; and defects should be classified as major/minor, root-caused, and remediated.

## 3. Topical synthesis

The 71 comments range from one-sentence statements to multi-page submissions with footnotes and attachments. They divide into three rough camps: election-administration professionals and good-government organizations who broadly support the draft and want it sharpened (e.g., The Elections Group, Verified Voting, Honest Elections Project, Center for Election Confidence, McBurnett, Rivest); auditors and accountants who judge the draft against professional auditing norms (Raisch, Vaeth, Schaeper, Chapman, Kresse, MFEI); and a large bloc of individual citizens — many submitting nearly identical one-liners — who want "third-party" or "independent" audits. A smaller set is skeptical of the whole enterprise or focused on specific grievances.

### Where there is broad agreement

**Auditor independence is the single most-cited theme.** It is the through-line of the citizen one-liners ("third party," "independent," "fox guarding the hen house") and the central argument of the most sophisticated submissions. Raisch calls independence the most important standard of all; Vaeth and Schaeper ground it in GAGAS/Yellow Book norms; MFEI, Buck, Koury, Watts, and Chapman all call for an independent audit committee or an Inspector-General-type body rather than the chief election official. Even supportive organizations (Verified Voting, The Elections Group) explicitly endorse a standard that auditors should not audit their own work. There is near-consensus that the entity running the election should not be the entity that designs, controls, and reports its own audit.

**Transparency and public observation are widely valued.** Commenters repeatedly ask for plain-language public reporting (Anonymous 0040, Center for Election Confidence, The Elections Group), meaningful observation (Droz, Citizens Oversight, Hyde), and published scope/criteria/results (Zarb-Cousin, Koury). MTX Group's only ask is to strengthen the document's tone on observation.

**Access to all records and proper reconciliation.** Several insist auditors must reach every artifact — paper ballots, cast vote records, chain-of-custody logs (Koury, Vaeth, MFEI, Schaeper, Vernesoni) — and that ballot counts must reconcile against ballots issued.

**The draft is a good and welcome starting point.** Even sharp critics (AFL, Droz, Citizens Oversight, Enhanced Voting) open by commending the effort; the disagreements are about what to add or how far it goes, not whether standards should exist at all.

### Where commenters are in tension

**Is independence achievable, and what does it mean?** The draft (and supporters like The Elections Group and Verified Voting) treats complete independence as often impractical, since election officials must retain custody of materials, and emphasizes mitigation. Many citizen commenters and the accountant bloc reject that framing as inadequate — Befort argues an audit that officials "plan, execute and report" is not independent at all. Verified Voting reframes independence functionally (auditors who cannot dictate outcomes), while Raisch relocates the problem upstream to who *controls* the audit rules.

**Risk-limiting audits: gold standard or misleading?** Rivest and McBurnett — both technical experts — fault the draft for *under*-emphasizing statistical/risk-limiting audits and want richer RLA guidance. Aleshire takes the opposite view, calling RLAs "statistically irrelevant and in fact misleading," and a strand of commenters (Klabechek, Cardarelli, 0072, parts of Droz) distrusts sampling and machines altogether, preferring full manual hand counts. This is the sharpest methodological split in the docket.

**Scope: narrow audit-process standards vs. broad election-integrity mandate.** AFL, Kufert, and Cruz (0070) push to expand the standards into citizenship verification, voter-roll integrity, drop-box security, signature verification, and non-citizen removal. Others would keep the document tightly about audit *process* and even narrow it: Enhanced Voting argues the EAC shouldn't write broad standards at all and should defer to GAO/INTOSAI; Lawson thinks the draft is already too long. The same facts (decentralization, varied state law) are read by some as a reason for flexibility and by others as a reason for firmer, more prescriptive minimums.

**Voluntary vs. mandatory; flexibility vs. firm minimums.** Honest Elections Project frames flexibility-vs-consistency as the core challenge; the draft leans toward flexibility. Anonymous 0072 and Chick want the standards effectively mandatory (or at least not labeled "voluntary"), and Schaeper/MFEI would tie HAVA funding to compliance. Droz warns that "Flexibility" can become an excuse to lower standards. Center for Election Confidence pulls the other way, wanting the word "standards" softened to "guide."

**Private funding.** The draft (and most commenters implicitly) favors public-only funding to protect independence; Anonymous 0042 is a notable dissent, arguing for safeguarded private funding as a fallback when public money falls short.

**Public vs. expert audience.** A recurring stylistic tension: Center for Election Confidence and Anonymous 0040 want language pitched to the average voter, while McBurnett, Rivest, and Citizens Oversight want more technical rigor and citations. The Elections Group's research is invoked to bridge this — process-focused, plain-language communication measurably raised confidence.

**Discrepancy handling — a shared gap.** Bystroff and Droz independently flag that the draft never says what happens when an audit finds a discrepancy, which both treat as the moment that matters most. This is less a disagreement than a widely under-addressed hole that several commenters want closed.

---

## 4. Conclusion

Read together, the 71 comments send the EAC a consistent high-level message: the draft is a credible and welcome framework, and its single most important principle — auditor independence — should be strengthened and made harder to evade. That message arrives from two directions at once: dozens of ordinary voters demanding plain "third-party" audits, and a smaller cohort of CPAs, auditors, statisticians, and election-integrity organizations making the same point in the vocabulary of GAGAS, segregation of duties, and conflict-of-interest. Where the draft treats full independence as often impractical, much of the docket treats it as the whole point.

Beyond independence, the comments converge on transparency, plain-language public reporting, complete auditor access to records, and reconciliation of ballots — and they surface one genuine gap that even supporters identify: the standards do not say what must happen when an audit finds a discrepancy.

The real tensions the Commission must navigate are not about whether to have standards but about their reach and rigor: statistical/risk-limiting methods versus full hand counts; a tight audit-process document versus a broad election-integrity mandate (citizenship, rolls, drop boxes, signatures); voluntary flexibility versus firm, possibly funding-linked minimums; and language written for experts versus for the average voter. A final version that tightens the independence and discrepancy-resolution language, adds clearer guidance on sampling and on what to do when numbers don't match, and keeps its plain-language, flexible posture while resisting the "voluntary means optional" critique would address the largest share of the substantive feedback — while inevitably leaving the deepest methodological dispute (trust in machines and statistical sampling versus hand counts) unresolved, because the commenters themselves do not agree on it.
