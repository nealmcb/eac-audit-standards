## How to read this document

This inventory has two parts:

1. **The summary table** below lists every commenter, the location/affiliation Regulations.gov captured, a single-sentence summary of what they said, and a Notes column for the type of submission (text, attachment, both) and noteworthy flags.
2. **The detailed entries** that follow expand each row into 1–3 paragraphs covering the substance of the comment, the principal recommendations, and the reasoning the submitter offered.

Commenters fall into a few rough clusters that recur throughout the dataset:
- A large block of short individual comments (often a single sentence) insisting audits must be performed by an independent third party rather than the Secretary of State or the elections office itself.
- A smaller set of detailed expert submissions — CPAs, election-security researchers, voting-system vendors, and advocacy organizations — proposing structural changes, redline edits, or whole alternative frameworks.
- A handful of off-topic or partisan submissions that nevertheless made it into the public record.

The Regulations.gov record does **not** publish submitter email addresses or phone numbers; the "Contact / location" column gives city/state/ZIP where those were captured, and organizational mailing addresses when the comment itself includes them.

Four ID numbers in the docket sequence — 0007, 0009, 0017, and 0024 — were present in the docket numbering totaling 71 active comments, but the underlying content could not be retrieved through the project's search index when this inventory was assembled. They appear at the end of the table with a note flagging that the full text should be pulled directly from Regulations.gov.

## Detailed entries

Entries are listed in the same order as the table above (comment ID ascending). Each entry gives the submitter, what they actually said, and — where relevant — the reasoning behind it.

### 1. EAC-2026-0067-0002 — Ronald Rivest (Arlington, MA 02476)

Rivest, a longtime academic voice on election security (and one of the original researchers behind risk-limiting audits), calls the draft "an excellent draft report" but identifies four substantive omissions. He notes the draft contains no discussion of statistical audits such as RLAs — including how to explain RLA results to a non-technical audience — and no acknowledgment that a statistical audit's workload is variable and depends on chance and the closeness of the contest. He also says the draft offers no advice on choosing *which* contests to audit when resources are limited, and no guidance on choosing ballots at random. Short, technical, and from someone whose name will carry weight with the Commission.

### 2. EAC-2026-0067-0003 — Stephanie Singer (El Cerrito, CA 94530)

A targeted one-line edit. Singer asks the EAC to add an "Anomaly Audit" row to the draft's example table of audit activities (Before/During/After an Election). Her proposed description: "An analysis of election data to identify statistical anomalies, with investigation to determine the causes of any significant anomalies." Singer is a known election-data analyst; the proposal complements Civera's comment (0005) and Rivest's RLA concern (0002).

### 3. EAC-2026-0067-0004 — Michelle Burchill (Prescott Valley, AZ 86314)

Speaks as a practitioner: "Working in the elections field for several years and cycles has given me more trust in the system." She nevertheless wants more audits, observing that many jurisdictions don't perform comprehensive audits unless something has already gone wrong. She specifically endorses the draft's "separate tabulation audit to ensure all ballots, not just a small sample, are being counted correctly." A rare insider voice among the public-comment record.

### 4. EAC-2026-0067-0005 — **Civera** (vendor)

Civera operates the ElectionShield product, which applies statistics to detect anomalies in election results. The company asks the EAC to make statistical/forensic analysis of results a standard security practice for all U.S. elections, with detected anomalies investigated in the field. To make the case for relevance the comment cites the federal conviction of former U.S. Congressman Ozzie Myers for ballot-box stuffing in Philadelphia. The comment doubles as product positioning; reviewers should evaluate the policy proposal separately from the vendor's commercial interest.

### 5. EAC-2026-0067-0006 — Gregory Lawson (Camano Island, WA 98282)

Lawson's first of two comments. He praises the work but says the document is too long and "gets into the weeds." His structural proposal: each state and county should create an Audit Committee made up of current and former election officials plus members-at-large, which determines audit details and hires an independent out-of-state auditor for each cycle, with the auditor changing every cycle and committee members rotating every two years. He lists the topics the audit should cover (internal controls, chain of custody, machine/technology accuracy, signature verification, registration, post-election tabulation) and suggests the EAC create a training program for independent election auditors as a new profession.

### 32. EAC-2026-0067-0037 — Gary Chick (San Antonio, TX 78247)

A longer, structured argument. ### 33. EAC-2026-0067-0038 — Richard Koury (OR 97527)

A structured seven-point proposal: (1) audits should be conducted and controlled by an independent Audit Committee separate from those conducting elections or maintaining voter rolls; (2) auditors must have access to all election records from original paper ballots through the Cast Vote Record, including chain-of-custody data; (3) the audit must reconcile voter counts and votes cast to the official record; (4) processes performed and any errors found must be documented and secured for future reference, and next year's planning should start by reviewing this year's documentation; (5) valid statistical methods set by the Audit Committee; (6) trained auditors; (7) a report to each county at completion. Among the more detailed lay-public submissions in the record.

### 34. EAC-2026-0067-0039 — Chris Bystroff (Troy, NY 12180)

The text in the CSV is a stub; the attached PDF/DOCX is the actual submission, and it is one of the most substantive in the record. Bystroff argues the draft contains a "critical gap" — it is silent on **discrepancy resolution**, that is, what an audit must do when the manual count and the machine tally disagree. He cites *Johnson v. Martins*, 30 Misc.3d 844 (NY Sup. Ct., Nassau County 2010), where a post-election audit discrepancy was deemed "reconcilable" because the administering authority offered a plausible explanation without any documentary corroboration. Bystroff's argument: "An explanation is a hypothesis. It describes what might have caused a discrepancy. It is not evidence that the hypothesized cause actually occurred." He maps three of the draft's existing standards (Impartiality, Independence, Appropriateness) onto the discrepancy-resolution problem and argues they should be extended explicitly to cover it, supported by GAO Yellow Book auditing doctrine on "Sufficient and Appropriate Evidence." A sophisticated legal/audit argument and arguably one of the technically strongest comments in the docket.

## Cross-cutting observations

A few patterns are worth noting at the docket level rather than per-comment:

**The dominant theme is independence.** Roughly 30 of the 71 comments — well over half of the individual (non-organizational) submissions — make essentially the same single point: audits must be done by an independent third party, not the Secretary of State, the elections office, or the vendor that supplied the system. This pattern is so strong that it likely reflects coordinated outreach to particular communities (notably Texas, with many San Antonio submissions), and several of the comments are nearly identical in length and wording.

**There is a much smaller — but proportionally influential — block of expert technical submissions.** Ronald Rivest (0002), Neal McBurnett (0069), Stephanie Singer (0003), and Chris Bystroff (0039) collectively push for the draft to engage more seriously with risk-limiting audits, sample-selection transparency, anomaly detection, and discrepancy resolution. These submissions are the ones most likely to drive concrete changes in the final standards.

**Three organizations submitted detailed structural reforms with overlapping ideas.** Michigan Fair Elections Institute (0044), Michael Raisch (0056, individual but treated as quasi-organizational by his endorsers), and America First Legal (0053) all push toward independent oversight, complete auditor access, and additional substantive audit topics — though they disagree on which topics are most urgent. MFEI and Raisch are explicitly endorsed by other commenters (0045, 0047, 0052); AFL's signature/citizenship/drop-box additions overlap with Kufert (0041).

**Three submissions implicitly or explicitly question the entire enterprise.** Enhanced Voting (0064) argues the EAC is duplicating GAO/INTOSAI work; the "Radical Legibility" comment (0071) argues for replacing digital tabulation entirely; and several of the third-party-independence comments treat the draft's voluntary, election-official-administered framing as fundamentally inadequate.

**A handful of submissions are off-topic, test entries, or single words** (0034, 0048, parts of 0027). They count toward the 71 but contribute little to the policy record.
